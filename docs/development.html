Play Harbor Deeds

What is an improvement worth?

Complete every deed in a district, then choose Improve before rolling. All current districts have three deeds. Membership, not a hard-coded count, determines completion. Under even building, a level-one deed cannot reach level two until both neighbors reach level one. Hosts may allow unrestricted building or buy one level across the whole district at its summed cost.

For a $100 deed with $25 base rent in Inner Harbor, rent moves from $50 to $75, $95 and $110. Successive purchases cost $10, $15 and $20. The $45 total buys $60 additional rent per paying visit; it does not add $45 to final wealth. Once a district is developed, none of its deeds can be traded. Bankruptcy clears its improvements.

Proof: marginal return decreases

Let C be the next purchase price and ΔR the increase over the previous level’s rent. For H expected future paying visits, expected incremental profit is HΔR − C. The break-even threshold is H* = C/ΔR. This is an expectation, not a promise of fractional visits. On the example deed the thresholds are 10/25 = 0.40, 15/20 = 0.75, and 20/15 = 1.33. Each dollar buys less extra rent at the next level. Already-spent acquisition costs are sunk for this decision, but are still required to unlock it.

The engine rounds each price and rent to the nearest dollar, halves up. Exhaustive evaluation of every shipped deed gives Inner thresholds of 0.400, 0.750 and 1.333. Outer ranges are [0.267, 0.300], [0.536, 0.567] and [0.906, 0.952]. The ranges do not overlap, so rounding cannot reverse the order. Under whole-district building the threshold is ΣC/ΣΔR, a weighted average of those thresholds with positive weights ΔR. Under even building, upgrading only a minimum-level deed preserves max level − min level ≤ 1 by induction from the all-zero initial state.

For a set of k deeds bought at list price, initial capital is ΣP; fully developing it costs an additional ΣC over all deeds and levels. For Old Town ($80, $100, $120), full Inner development costs $135 on top of $300 in deeds. Adding a fourth deed to the data would require acquiring and leveling that fourth deed too. Actual auction and trade prices change the capital invested, not future construction quotes.

Why three levels and different board prices?

With uniform positions on N spaces, m solvent opponents and T rolls each, expected visits to one deed are mT/N. A 28-space board therefore earns roughly 20/28 as many visits per deed as a 20-space board. Outer construction percentages approximate this factor. With two opponents, the Inner tiers need roughly 4, 7.5 and 13.3 remaining rounds at uniform occupancy. Tier three is for early sets, not a routine last-round purchase. A fourth, lower-return tier would compete for an even smaller slice of an 18-round game.

Uniform positions are a benchmark, not an opening-game assumption. The bot convolves current opponent positions with the actual two-dice distribution for each remaining roll, using Pr(sum=d) = (6−|7−d|)/36. It buys only if predicted added rent exceeds cost and it retains a cash reserve. That forecast assumes opponents survive and pay in full; actual bankruptcies can lower proceeds.

An initial 15/25/40% schedule produced no level-three purchases on either board under even building. Using the final Inner prices on Outer still produced zero; the board-specific schedule produced 19. The final curve makes all tiers usable, with bankruptcy more frequent than in the control. Identical counts for even and unrestricted suggest the current bots already favor spreading investment; they do not prove the policies equivalent for human players. Neither the algebra nor these games prove optimal balance or enjoyment.
