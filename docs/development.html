Play Harbor Deeds

Build, refit, or keep the cash?

On Grand Harbor, permanently improving Copper Yard now costs $28 for +$35 rent per paying visit, up from $10. One full hit leaves $7 more cash than holding; no hit leaves $28 less. Construction also takes the whole Foundry district off the sale and trade market. Neither the building nor a temporary refit counts toward the final score.

The permanent curve

Own every deed in the district to collect set rent and build before rolling. Even building allows a lowest-level deed; unrestricted building allows any deed below the cap. Whole-district building purchases the next level everywhere at its summed price. All shipped groups contain three deeds, but the engine uses actual membership.

For Copper Yard ($140 list, $35 base), old permanent rents were $70 → $105 → $133 → $154; successive costs were $10, $15, $20. The revised path is $70 → $105 → $137 → $163 for $28, $29, $31. The second and third purchases buy only the increment over the previous rent. Building out this one deed now takes $88 rather than $45, plus the neighboring construction required by the policy. Auction and trade prices are sunk costs for this decision.

Let C be the next price, ΔR the added rent and H the expected number of fully paid future visits. Ignoring other effects, the expected cash change versus holding is HΔR − C. Break-even is H* = C/ΔR. At uniform occupancy, two surviving rivals with 13 rolls each on Grand give H = 26/40 = 0.65. On Copper's first tier that benchmark changes from +$12.75 to −$5.25. It takes about 16 rolls per rival to reach the new threshold at uniform occupancy, not six. Actual dice positions, visits, solvency and remaining turns matter.

A 28-space, 18-round board offers fewer visits per deed than Inner in the same time; Grand runs for 26 rounds.

One paying visit instead of a permanent level

Dock Works (#20) and Rigging Works (#35) replace two of Grand's seven Dispatch spaces. On landing, commission one owned deed with no outstanding refit, even in a split district, or skip for $0. The fee is half the deed's base rent, rounded up on halves; the bonus is one base rent. On Copper, pay $18 now for a chance at an extra $35 once. The chance of at least one fully paid hit must exceed 18/35 (51.4%) for positive cash value. Three visits still earn only one bonus, whereas a permanent level increases every rent. Works is not available on demand.

The next rival rent visit consumes the bonus, including a Guild-covered or partially paid visit; the owner’s own visits do not. It stacks with permanent rent, not another refit. It does not raise a permanent level, unlock a set bonus, count as a level for cards, or lock the district. Sale, transfer or bankruptcy cancels that deed's refit without reimbursement. Cards, guilds and exchanges remain on the revised board.

Cash now, wealth later

Before rolling, the current player can return an eligible deed to the bank. The default quote is 50% of printed list: win a $200 deed for $60 and the bank offers $100, not $30. That can profit against the auction bid, but the sale also removes a $200 asset from final wealth, stops rent income and can break a set. No trade proposal is used up. There is no emergency sale once a bill is due.

Designers can instead use the actual bank acquisition basis: direct purchase records list, auction records the winning bid. A subsequent player trade, including a cash-and-deed swap, keeps that receipt; under 50% acquisition the same deed still quotes $30. Return to the bank clears the receipt; reacquisition records a fresh one. A permanent improvement on any district deed prevents both sale and trade of every member, including unbuilt siblings. Bankruptcy returns the defaulter’s deeds and clears their levels, refits and receipts.

Grand's even-building bots bought 287 permanent levels across 100 three-seat games, down from 1,264 in the old-curve control. Tier three is now rare at three seats and common at four; the result depends strongly on traffic. Identical counts for even and unrestricted do not make those rules equivalent. In the revised three-seat even sample, 385 of 402 Works landings had an affordable target. Bots commissioned 179, of which 165 were on split sets; 90 bonuses were triggered before game end. A trigger does not guarantee full payment. Bots also sold 349 deeds to the bank. These are actions on the same simulated tables, not independent human preferences.

Same decision, opposite choices

Across 200 Grand seeds (1–200), three seats, even policy, take the first pre-roll state with an affordable permanent upgrade under the revised prices. Replay its actual history under both curves. No prior build has happened, so both versions have the same cash, positions, ownership and candidate. All 200 seeds qualified; the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of the decision round were 6, 10 and 17. In one branch buy the first quoted affordable level then roll; in the other roll immediately. All subsequent actions in each branch use its curve and the same bot policy. Holding this turn does not ban future builds.

Four revised-curve builders went bankrupt while their paired holders survived; none showed the reverse. Shared wins count as wins. Large tails reflect later auctions, defaults and opponent cash, not thousands of dollars of marginal rent on the first deed. Holding more often finished richer, but neither choice dominated. The many ties and a median of zero are important: this is not a forecast of the payoff for an arbitrary human decision.

Limits. Bots forecast visits from current positions and the full configured dice-sum distribution, assuming solvent opponents, full payment and play through the round limit. For a one-use bonus they estimate the probability of at least one visit, not the expected number of visits. They reserve up to one salary or one third of cash, whichever is less. In a duel, unprotected rent can change the score difference by twice its value: the bot credits that effect when its only rival has no cover. A defensive sale considers known next-roll fees and rival rents if the estimated default risk is at least 10%; it does not forecast every event, nor find the globally optimal portfolio. Ledger thresholds ignore denial value, two-way transfers, card effects, insolvency, opportunity costs and future trades. The hand-picked fork is not a random sample of all late-game positions; matching seeds is not a playtest or proof of optimal balance.

The scripts use the release WASM and source JSON, not a possibly stale published page. web/rules.json sets Inner development, set bonus and bank_sale: {rate_pct: 50, basis: "list"}; use "acquisition" for the receipt basis. The rate accepts integer percentages 0–100; omitting the bank rule disables bank sales (as on frozen legacy tables). web/outer-harbor.json overrides the classic expansion's construction prices. web/grand-harbor.json sets its own full curve and the Works spaces; their cost_pct and bonus_pct apply to the target deed's printed base rent, not its price or its current total rent.
